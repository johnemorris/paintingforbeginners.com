## Content collisions and relationships

### Articles with substantially overlapping primary intent

There are no exact duplicates, but the following are the closest primary-intent collisions and should be treated as collision risks during demand research:

1. **`do-you-need-primer` ↔ `paint-and-primer-in-one-do-you-still-need-primer`**  
   Both answer whether separate primer is required and cover the same core exceptions: bare/porous substrate, repairs, stains, slick surfaces, adhesion, and difficult changes. The latter is a branded-claim/product-label variant of the former rather than a wholly separate decision.

2. **`painting-supplies-for-beginners` ↔ the supply/setup portions of `acrylic-painting-for-beginners`**  
   The standalone supply guide and the first-canvas guide both specify essentially the same compact acrylic setup. Their nominal primary intents differ (buying a kit versus making a first painting), but the generic title of the supply guide increases the likelihood that both compete for broad beginner-supplies queries.

3. **`room-painting-supply-checklist` ↔ the planning/supply portions of `how-to-paint-a-room`**  
   Both help a first-time room painter assemble what is needed. The checklist is much more inventory-led and the room guide is process-led, so this is a manageable collision if those roles remain explicit.

### Partial overlap with legitimately different primary intent

- **Preparation cluster:** `surface-preparation-before-painting` is the general diagnostic framework; `can-you-paint-over-old-paint`, `do-you-need-to-sand-before-painting`, and `do-you-need-primer` answer individual decisions inside it. ### Obvious gaps mentioned or implied by existing articles

These are boundaries or follow-on needs already exposed by the current articles, not a recommendation to create one article for every category slot.

- **Repairs larger than “small drywall damage.”** `patch-nail-holes-and-small-drywall-damage` explicitly limits its method and tells the reader to recognize when damage is no longer a small fill-and-sand repair. The collection has no corresponding treatment of larger holes, tape/joint failures, or damaged drywall sections.
- **Veneer-specific furniture decisions.** `how-to-paint-wood-furniture` teaches readers to distinguish solid wood, veneer, and laminate, while the collection provides material-specific paths only for wood and laminate. Veneer's thin face layer and sanding limits are acknowledged but not developed as a dedicated workflow.
- **Powered paint spraying.** `paint-brush-vs-roller` discusses powered sprayers and when setup might be worthwhile, but the only spray technique guide covers aerosol cans. Masking, thinning/product compatibility, equipment setup, pattern adjustment, cleanup, and overspray control for powered equipment remain outside the current detailed coverage.
- **Moisture-source and systemic adhesion diagnosis.** `why-is-paint-peeling-or-bubbling` makes moisture the first diagnostic branch and appropriately tells readers when to stop DIY diagnosis, but the collection does not go deeper into distinguishing condensation, plumbing, roof/exterior intrusion, or substrate moisture. This may remain a deliberate boundary because it crosses into building diagnosis.
- **Older-paint/lead-safe preparation.** Both sanding guides and the preparation/failure material raise an older-paint safety warning, but the collection does not provide a comprehensive lead-safe decision or work-practice resource. This is an implied safety boundary, not simply a topical vacancy.
- **Acrylic color-mixing and value control.** The first-canvas and landscape guides both rely on a limited palette, mixing, lighter/less intense distant colors, and broad value zones. Those concepts are introduced in-project but not explained beyond the immediate exercise.
- **Acrylic brush handling beyond one project.** The canvas guides mention brush size, broad shapes, layering, edges, and cleanup, but the detailed brush-loading and mark-control articles elsewhere are written for architectural coatings rather than artist acrylic technique.
- **Complex stains and contamination.** The primer, surface-preparation, and peeling guides repeatedly distinguish stain blocking and contamination from ordinary coverage, but they do not provide a source-by-source treatment of common household stains or residues. The current collection correctly keeps the general articles conditional rather than pretending one cleanup method fits all.
- **Caulk-selection and joint decisions.** The trim guide tells beginners to fill only unwanted damage and use flexible caulk where appropriate, but the collection does not deeply explain which joints should be caulked, left open, repaired differently, or allowed to move.
