A roller is simple enough to use badly on the first try.

The most common beginner mistake is treating it like a tool that must be pressed against the wall. A roller works best when the cover is carrying enough paint and you guide it across the surface with controlled pressure.

## 1. Start with the right cover

Choose the roller-cover material and nap for both:

- the paint manufacturer's recommendation; and
- the actual surface texture.

A very short nap can skip low spots on a textured wall. A long nap on a smooth wall can leave more stipple than you want.

If the cover manufacturer instructs you to remove loose fibers or lightly condition the cover before use, do that before loading paint.

## 2. Stir the paint

Pigment and other components can settle during storage. Stir thoroughly from the bottom until the paint is uniform.

If you are using several cans of the same color on one large area, combining them in a larger container can reduce small batch differences.

## 3. Load the roller evenly

Pour paint into a tray reservoir or use a bucket and roller grid.

Roll the cover into the paint, then across the tray ramp or grid several times. Rotate the roller as you load it.

The goal is an evenly saturated cover that is **not dripping**.

A roller that is wet on one side and nearly dry on the other will put uneven paint on the wall.

## 4. Put the first stroke in a dry area

Begin near—not directly on—the wet edge of the previous section, then work back toward it.

This gives the fresh load somewhere to go before you blend into the existing paint.

You may see painters describe W, M, or N patterns. The letter matters less than the principle: distribute the load over a manageable area, then even it out without repeatedly working paint that is beginning to dry.

## 5. Use overlapping passes

Work a section roughly a few feet wide at a time, adjusting for your reach, wall size, and drying conditions.

Overlap each pass enough to avoid gaps. Keep the roller moving smoothly and maintain a wet edge into the next section.

Do not leave a thick ridge at the end of each pass.

## 6. Reload sooner than you think

Listen and look.

When the roller begins to sound dry, requires extra pressure, or leaves broken coverage, reload it.

Do not try to "get your money's worth" from every roller load by pressing harder. That can leave thin paint in the center, heavy ridges at the edges, and uneven texture.

## 7. Finish with light, consistent passes

After distributing paint across the section, use light finishing passes in the same direction to even the texture.

Do not squeeze the roller into the wall. Keep the frame aligned so one edge is not carrying much more pressure than the other.

Then move on.

## 8. Don't go back once the paint starts setting

This is one of the hardest habits for beginners.

You notice a small spot behind you. You roll over it. Then you see another. Soon you are dragging a roller through paint that is no longer flowing normally.

If the paint has started to tack, leave it alone. Let the coat dry and correct the spot during the next coat.

## How close should you roll to the edges?

Use a brush to cut in the narrow areas the roller cannot safely reach, then bring the roller reasonably close while that brushed paint is still wet.

This helps the brush and roller textures blend.

Do not slam the roller end into trim or a ceiling edge. Paint can squeeze from the end of the cover and create a heavy ridge.

## Using an extension pole

An extension pole can improve both reach and consistency on walls and ceilings because you can use longer strokes without repeatedly climbing a ladder.

Use only a length you can control comfortably. Lock adjustable poles securely. Keep the work area clear and follow ladder safety whenever a ladder is still required.

## Practice before the most visible wall

If this is your first room, practice on a section that will be partly hidden by furniture or on a prepared sample panel.

Learn how much paint the roller holds, how far one load travels, and what the surface looks like after drying.

## Five signs your technique is working

- The roller turns freely instead of sliding.
- You are not pressing hard.
- Coverage stays solid until you reload.
- Each new section blends into a wet edge.
- You can move forward without repeatedly repairing the area behind you.

The goal is not to roll as fast as possible. It is to leave one consistent paint film and then give it time to level and dry.
