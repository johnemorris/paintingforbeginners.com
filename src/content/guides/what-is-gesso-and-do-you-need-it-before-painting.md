Gesso (pronounced “JESS-oh”) is a preparatory ground applied to an art support before painting. Modern acrylic gesso typically combines an acrylic binder, white pigment, and mineral solids that create tooth—the slight texture paint can grip. It is not the same as ordinary white paint.

## Do you need gesso?

Usually **no extra gesso is required on a factory pre-primed canvas**. It is already prepared for the media listed by the manufacturer. You can begin acrylic painting directly unless you want a smoother, rougher, tinted, or less absorbent surface.

You generally **do need an appropriate ground on raw canvas or an unprepared absorbent panel**. The complete preparation depends on the support and paint medium. Oil painting on natural-fiber canvas requires particular sizing and ground considerations to isolate fibers from oil; use an established system intended for oil rather than assuming one coat of any acrylic product is sufficient.

## Gesso, primer, and white paint are not interchangeable

House-paint primer is formulated for architectural substrates and topcoats. Artist gesso is formulated as a ground for art materials. White acrylic paint supplies color and binder but may not provide the tooth, absorbency, or solids of gesso.

Use products as labeled. “Primer” is a broad job description, not proof that any primer suits any artwork.

## When an extra coat helps

Add gesso to a compatible pre-primed support when you want to:

- cover an overly slick or uneven factory ground;
- reduce prominent canvas texture with several thin, sanded coats;
- create more tooth for visible dry-brush marks;
- tint the ground so you do not begin on bright white; or
- make several supports behave more consistently.

Test first. Added gesso changes how paint moves and may obscure a canvas weave you liked.

## Prepare common supports

### Pre-primed stretched canvas or canvas panel

Remove dust and inspect for dents, loose fabric, oily contamination, or damaged ground. If sound, paint directly. If adding gesso, confirm compatibility, apply thin coats, and do not allow material to pool along edges.

### Raw canvas

Stretching, sizing, and grounding raw fabric is a materials project in itself. Use a system intended for the fiber and painting medium, following its coat and drying instructions. Beginners who want to paint now will usually get a more predictable result from pre-primed canvas.

### Wood panel

Use a stable panel intended for artwork. Seal and ground all required faces and edges according to the panel and ground manufacturers; this can reduce uneven absorption but does not prevent all movement. Sand edges safely and remove dust.

### Paper

Heavy acrylic or mixed-media paper may accept acrylic without gesso. Gesso changes its absorbency and can cause lighter paper to buckle. Secure or stretch the paper as appropriate and make a sample.

## How to apply acrylic gesso

1. Confirm the support is clean, dry, sound, and compatible.
2. Stir gently. Thin only if the manufacturer permits it.
3. Apply an even coat with a wide brush, roller, or other recommended tool.
4. Let it dry for the stated time.
5. Apply another thin coat in a different direction if needed.
6. For a smooth surface, sand only when fully dry and when the product allows it; control dust and avoid inhalation.
7. Remove dust, then wait the required time before painting.

Brush marks in gesso are not inherently wrong. Preserve them when they support the planned painting; level them when they would interfere with fine detail.

## Common misconceptions

- More coats are not always better; they add labor and alter the surface.
- Gesso does not repair a loose, warped, moldy, or contaminated support.
- Gesso is not a final protective varnish.
- A pre-primed canvas is not “cheating”; it is a prepared support.
- Clear gesso is not optically invisible in every application; its solids can create a cloudy or gritty appearance.
