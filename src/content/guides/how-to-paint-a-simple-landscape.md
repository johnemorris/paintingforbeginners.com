A landscape is a good first subject because it can remain recognizable without precise drawing. This project uses four layers: sky, distant land, nearer ground, and selected foreground marks.

## Choose and crop a reference

Use a reference with a clear horizon and only a few major shapes. Crop it to the same proportion as your canvas. Decide whether the sky or land gets more space; placing the horizon exactly halfway can make both feel equally important.

Do not copy every tree or cloud. Identify the lightest area, darkest area, and main direction of the land.

## 1. Mix a small set of colors

Make a sky color, a lighter version for the horizon, a muted distant-land color, and a darker foreground mixture. Save some base colors so you can adjust as you work.

Test mixtures on scrap paper. Distant colors generally look lighter, cooler, and less contrasty than foreground colors because of atmospheric haze.

## 2. Paint the sky

Use the flat brush and work from the top toward the horizon. Place the stronger sky color first, then blend the lighter horizon mixture into it while both are wet. Use broad horizontal strokes and stop before repeated brushing makes the gradient chalky or streaky.

Clouds are optional. If you add them, vary their size and edge softness. A row of identical white bumps reads as symbols rather than clouds.

## 3. Add distant land

Let the sky dry if you want a clean ridge. Draw the hill or tree line with a simple uneven edge; avoid making a regular zigzag. Fill the distant shape with the muted mixture.

Keep details low. Distant trees can be suggested by groups and edge changes instead of individual trunks and branches.

## 4. Paint the middle and foreground

Add a nearer field, shoreline, or hill with slightly stronger color and contrast. Then place the darkest large foreground shape. Overlap layers so the scene has depth.

If a path or river moves into the distance, make it narrower as it approaches the horizon. Check the whole shape before outlining its edges.

## 5. Add a focal point

Choose one place for the viewer to pause: a small tree, a light path, a cabin shape, or a bright patch of field. Give that area a clearer edge or stronger light-dark difference than its surroundings.

Use the small brush late, and sparingly. A few purposeful branches or grass marks are more convincing than an even fringe around every shape.

> **If colors turn muddy:** rinse the brush, refresh the mixture, and let the layer dry. Trying harder with a dirty brush usually mixes more of the unwanted color.

## 6. Finish by removing, not adding

Step back several feet. If the scene reads, soften any accidental hard line that steals attention. Repeat one or two colors in small amounts to connect the layers. Then stop and let the work dry before deciding it needs more.

Sign with a small mark only after the surface is dry enough that your hand will not drag through paint. The first landscape is a record of decisions, not an exam; paint the same scene again later and notice how much easier the sequence feels.
