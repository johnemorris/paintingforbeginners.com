Acrylic is a friendly first painting medium because it cleans up with water while wet, dries quickly, and can cover earlier layers. Its speed can also surprise beginners, so a simple setup and a small painting are more useful than a giant box of supplies.

## Choose a first subject you can simplify

Pick something with a few large shapes: a sunset, simple landscape, fruit, leaves, or an abstract arrangement. Avoid choosing a reference whose appeal depends on tiny facial details or perfect perspective.

Your goal is to practice arranging shapes, making a few colors, and covering a canvas—not to prove that you are “good at art.”

## Set up before opening paint

Protect the table and your clothes. Put the reference where you can see it without touching wet work. If you paint with your right hand, keeping water and palette to the right often reduces reaching across the canvas; reverse that if you are left-handed.

Use two water containers: one for the first brush rinse and a cleaner one for the final rinse. Keep a rag nearby. Acrylic can dry in a brush, so do not leave a loaded brush sitting in open air or standing on its bristles.

## 1. Limit your palette

A warm red, yellow, blue, white, and a dark color can do a great deal. Fewer colors make it easier to understand what each mixture is doing. Put out small amounts; you can add more.

Mix with the brush or a palette knife using deliberate scoops rather than stirring every color together. Make enough of an important mixture to finish its area. Recreating an exact color after it is gone is harder than beginners expect.

## 2. Mark the largest shapes

Lightly sketch only the main divisions. For a landscape, that may be the horizon, the silhouette of a hill, and one large foreground shape. Too much drawing creates lines you feel obligated to fill in.

Check proportions from a few feet away. Big relationships matter before small details.

## 3. Paint broad areas first

Use the largest brush that comfortably fits the shape. Cover the background and large color areas with controlled, even strokes. A slightly damp—not dripping—brush can help the first layer move, but too much water weakens coverage and makes paint run.

If the canvas texture shows, decide whether you like it. You do not need to force thick paint into every low spot on the first pass.

## 4. Use drying to your advantage

Wet colors blend softly. Dry layers allow crisp edges and corrections. If two neighboring colors keep becoming muddy, let the first one dry before adding the second.

Acrylic often dries darker or less bright than it looks wet. Judge color after a test mixture dries when the exact value matters.

## 5. Add medium shapes, then details

Once the big areas work, add trees, shadows, windows, flower centers, or other medium shapes. Save the smallest brush and brightest highlights for the end. Details cannot repair a weak overall arrangement, and too many equally sharp details make the viewer unsure where to look.

Step back often. A painting viewed only from six inches away becomes a collection of local problems.

## 6. Know when to stop

Ask whether the subject reads clearly and whether one area attracts attention. If so, pause. Let the canvas dry and look again tomorrow. You can add another layer; undoing a dozen unnecessary marks is harder.

## Clean brushes immediately

Wipe excess paint onto a rag, rinse, and wash brushes with mild soap while the acrylic is still wet. Work soap gently through the bristles, rinse until clear, reshape, and dry flat or bristles-down where water will not collect in the ferrule. Do not pour solid paint or sludge down the drain; let residue settle and follow local disposal guidance.

## What to practice next

Make several small paintings instead of protecting one canvas from mistakes. Repeat the same subject with a different color mood, or paint a simple object under different light. Repetition turns a mysterious process into a set of choices you recognize.
