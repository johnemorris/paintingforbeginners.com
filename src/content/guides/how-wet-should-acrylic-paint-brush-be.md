For ordinary acrylic painting, a useful starting point is clean and slightly damp—not dripping wet.

But brush moisture is a technique control, not a rule. A dry-brush effect needs less water. A loose wash may need more.

## Start by wetting, then removing the excess

When beginning a session:

1. Wet the clean brush.
2. Gently remove excess water.
3. Load paint.
4. Watch how the stroke behaves.

If water runs down the handle or pools on the painting, the brush is wetter than you need for ordinary opaque painting.

## Signs the brush may be too dry

- paint drags before you want it to;
- the stroke breaks apart;
- the brush cannot release paint smoothly; or
- acrylic starts drying in the bristles during normal use.

That does not always mean “add water.” You may need more paint, a different paint consistency, a larger brush, or a faster workflow.

## Signs the brush may be too wet

- paint becomes unexpectedly transparent;
- color runs or pools;
- the stroke loses body;
- water drips from the ferrule; or
- you cannot control the edge.

Blot excess water and reload. If you mean to thin the paint itself, use the separate [water-in-acrylic decision](/can-you-add-water-to-acrylic-paint/).

## Keep brushes from drying while you work

Acrylic can dry in a brush quickly. Rinse brushes during the session before paint hardens in the bristles, especially when changing colors or setting a brush aside.

Do not leave brushes standing bristle-down in water for long periods. Use a simple [rinse workflow that keeps acrylic from drying in brushes](/how-to-keep-acrylic-paint-from-drying-in-brushes/).

The right amount of water is the amount that gives you the stroke you intend without accidentally turning the paint into a wash.
