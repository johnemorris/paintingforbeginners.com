Art-store aisles make a simple hobby look as if it needs a workshop. It does not. Buy enough to learn what you enjoy, then upgrade the things whose limitations you can actually notice.

## Surface

Choose a pre-primed canvas panel, stretched canvas, or acrylic-compatible paper. A modest panel is inexpensive and easy to store. Acrylic paper works well for exercises, but check its weight and whether it is designed to accept wet media.

Avoid raw, unprimed canvas for the first project unless preparing the surface is specifically what you want to learn.

## Paint

Student-grade acrylic is a sensible starting point. A small selection teaches mixing better than a huge set of nearly identical colors. Look for clearly named colors and consistent availability so you can replace a useful tube later.

A basic working palette includes a red, yellow, blue, white, and a dark neutral. Exact pigments affect mixing, but you do not need to solve every color-theory question before your first canvas.

## Brushes

Three brushes can cover most first projects:

1. A medium flat brush for backgrounds and straight-edged shapes.
2. A medium round brush for flexible shapes and lines.
3. A small round brush for selected details.

Choose handles that feel comfortable and bristles intended for acrylic. Very cheap brushes that shed, splay immediately, or cannot return to a point create frustration. You still do not need professional-priced brushes to begin.

## Palette and water

A reusable plastic palette, coated paper plate, or palette paper can hold paint. Avoid very absorbent bare paper, which pulls moisture from acrylic and tears during mixing.

Use two water containers: a dirty first rinse and a cleaner final rinse. Wide, stable containers are harder to tip. Do not use food containers that might later return to kitchen use.

## Protection and cleanup

Cover the work surface and wear clothes that can be stained. Acrylic becomes water-resistant when dry and can be difficult or impossible to remove from fabric. Keep a rag or paper towel within reach.

Mild soap is generally enough for brush cleanup while paint is wet. Specialty brush cleaner can wait unless your chosen product directs otherwise.

## Useful later, not essential now

- **Easel:** Helpful for upright work, but a supported tabletop setup is fine.
- **Palette knife:** Useful for mixing and textured painting after you know you want those techniques.
- **Mediums:** Change drying, transparency, texture, or flow. Learn the paint first and use products formulated for acrylic rather than household substitutes.
- **Varnish:** A separate skill with compatibility and curing requirements; not part of finishing your first practice canvas.
- **Large color sets:** Convenient, but they can postpone learning a few dependable mixtures.

## Spend where it reduces friction

If the budget permits, prioritize paint that covers predictably, brushes that keep their shape, and a surface that stays flat. A branded “starter kit” is only useful if its contents match the project. Compare the actual brush sizes, paint quantities, and surface rather than treating the word “beginner” as a quality guarantee.
