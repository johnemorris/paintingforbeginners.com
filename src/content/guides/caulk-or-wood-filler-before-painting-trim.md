A simple way to choose is to ask whether you are filling a defect in the trim itself or a joint between two surfaces.

Use a compatible filler for nail holes, dents, gouges, and other defects that should become part of a rigid smooth surface.

Use paintable caulk for appropriate narrow joints where two surfaces meet and a little movement needs to be accommodated.

## Think “hole” versus “joint”

Typical filler jobs include nail holes, small dents, shallow gouges, and damaged spots that need to be sanded flush.

Typical caulk jobs include a narrow gap between painted trim and a wall, certain trim-to-trim joints intended to appear continuous, and small joints where a flexible seal is appropriate.

## Why not fill everything with caulk?

Caulk is not a universal hole filler. It generally does not sand and shape like a rigid filler, and a blob placed into a dent can remain visibly soft or irregular.

Likewise, rigid filler is a poor substitute for a joint that needs flexibility.

## Not every gap should automatically be caulked

Large gaps, moving assemblies, drainage details, exterior joints, or evidence of installation problems may require a different repair.

If you do not understand why the gap exists, especially around windows, doors, wet areas, or exterior assemblies, do not seal it blindly. First decide [whether the gap is appropriate to caulk](/what-gaps-should-you-caulk-before-painting/).

## Finish the repair for paint

For filler, allow it to dry as directed, sand it flush if required, remove dust, and prime exposed porous material when needed.

For caulk, use a product compatible with the joint and intended to be painted when the finished joint will receive paint. Respect its cure and paint-ready instructions.
