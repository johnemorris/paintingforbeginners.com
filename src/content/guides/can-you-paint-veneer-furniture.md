Yes, many veneer pieces can be painted—but first make sure you actually have veneer and that the veneer is sound.

Veneer is a thin layer of real wood applied over another material. That makes preparation different from aggressively sanding a thick piece of solid wood.

## Inspect before sanding

Look for:

- lifting or loose veneer;
- bubbles;
- chipped or missing areas;
- water damage;
- swollen substrate;
- previous coatings or polish; and
- edges where the thin face layer is visible.

Loose veneer needs repair before painting. Paint will not glue a failing surface back down.

## Clean thoroughly

Furniture polish, wax, grease, and hand oils can interfere with adhesion. Clean the piece with a method compatible with the existing finish and intended coating system.

## Sand lightly and for a reason

The goal is usually to smooth defects and dull a slick existing finish—not to remove the veneer.

Because veneer is thin, aggressive sanding can cut through the face and expose the substrate underneath. Use the coating manufacturer's preparation instructions and err toward controlled scuffing rather than stripping away material unnecessarily.

## Prime when the system needs it

A bonding or stain-blocking primer may be appropriate depending on the existing finish, wood extractives, repairs, and finish paint.

Do not choose primer merely because “furniture always needs primer.” Choose it to solve an actual adhesion, stain, porosity, or system requirement. The [primer guide](/do-you-need-primer/) explains those jobs.

## Apply controlled coats and protect the cure

Furniture sees hands, objects, friction, and cleaning. Thin, uniform coats followed by the required recoat and cure time are more useful than trying to make the piece look finished in one heavy application.

Painted furniture can feel dry long before it is ready for hard use, so respect [dry, recoat, and cure time](/paint-dry-recoat-cure-time/).
