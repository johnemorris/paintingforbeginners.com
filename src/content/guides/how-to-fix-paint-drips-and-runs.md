A drip happens when more paint collects in one spot than the coating can hold evenly.

The fix depends on one important question:

**Is the paint still workable, or has it started to set?**

  <figcaption>A run is excess film that has moved downward. Its wetness—not its size alone—determines whether to spread it now or level it after drying.</figcaption>
</figure>

## If you catch the drip immediately

If the surrounding paint is still wet and flowing normally, lightly redistribute the excess with the appropriate brush or roller.

Use as few strokes as necessary.

Then leave it alone.

Repeatedly poking at the area as it begins drying can turn one drip into a much larger patch of texture.

## If the drip is tacky or partly dry

Stop.

This is the stage where beginners often make the problem worse. A skin may have formed over softer paint underneath, and brushing it can tear or wrinkle the finish.

Let the coating dry according to its directions before attempting a repair.

## If the drip has dried

Once the coating is hard enough to work without tearing:

1. level the raised drip carefully using a fine abrasive or another method appropriate to the coating
2. feather the edges rather than digging a crater around the defect
3. remove the dust
4. spot-prime if your repair exposes a substrate that requires it
5. recoat the area or natural break uniformly

A severe run may require more than a quick sanding pass.

## Why drips happen

Common causes include:

- overloading the brush
- carrying too much paint on the roller
- applying a coat too heavily
- paint collecting at corners or trim profiles
- failing to check lower edges after brushing
- trying to make one coat do the work of several

## Check the surface before moving on

Before moving too far away, glance back at freshly painted edges, corners, and areas below heavy brush work.

A drip is easiest to correct while the entire area is still genuinely wet.

## Do not solve drips by starving the tools

Using almost no paint creates a different set of problems: poor hiding, dry roller texture, and excessive overworking.

The goal is a **properly loaded tool and a uniform film**, not the least possible paint.
