Yes, water can be useful with acrylic paint—but water changes the paint, and there is no universal percentage that is right for every acrylic, surface, and technique.

A little water on the brush can improve handling. Larger amounts make the paint thinner and more transparent. If your goal is substantial thinning while maintaining the paint's binder and working properties, an acrylic medium is often the better tool.

## Start with the result you want

Ask why you are adding water.

### To keep a brush comfortably damp

Use a small amount. The brush should not be dripping unless the technique specifically calls for it.

### To make a slightly looser stroke

Add water gradually and test.

### To make a transparent wash

Water may be part of the technique, but the appropriate amount depends on the paint and surface. Follow the paint manufacturer's guidance.

### To dramatically thin or extend the paint

Use a medium designed for that purpose rather than assuming unlimited water is harmless.

## Why too much water can be a problem

Acrylic paint contains pigment held in an acrylic binder. Adding a lot of water spreads that binder and pigment farther apart and can reduce color strength and change the resulting film.

Manufacturers formulate different acrylic lines differently, so use their instructions when making heavily diluted applications.

## Medium is not just expensive water

Acrylic mediums contain compatible binder and are designed to change flow, transparency, gloss, drying behavior, or body while maintaining the acrylic system.

You do not need a shelf full of mediums to begin painting. But if you repeatedly find yourself adding lots of water just to make your paint behave differently, the right medium may solve the problem more predictably.
