Painting is the act of applying a colored or clear coating to a surface. People paint to protect buildings and objects, change how spaces feel, identify or communicate, and make images. The same word covers coating a wall and making an artwork, but both depend on matching material, surface, preparation, and technique.

## Why do humans paint?

Some painting is **functional**: exterior paint limits weather exposure, a cabinet coating tolerates handling, and markings communicate information. Some is **decorative**: a wall color changes a room or a finish renews furniture. Artistic painting uses color, shape, value, texture, and composition to express or observe something. A project can serve more than one purpose.

## What is paint made from?

Most paints contain three basic parts:

- **Pigment** supplies color and often opacity. Not every coating is heavily pigmented; clear finishes protect without hiding the surface.
- **Binder** is the film-forming material that holds pigment and attaches the dry coating to the surface. Acrylic polymer and drying oils are examples.
- **Carrier or solvent** helps the paint flow during application, then leaves as the coating dries. In waterborne paint, water is a major carrier.

Manufacturers also use additives to adjust flow, drying, mildew resistance, storage, texture, or other properties. “Water-based” does not mean paint is harmless to every surface or safe to pour down a drain; use, ventilation, cleanup, and disposal still follow the label and local rules.

## The surface is part of the paint system

Painters call the material being painted the **substrate**. Drywall, plaster, wood, metal, masonry, laminate, paper, canvas, and plastic absorb and move differently. A coating that performs on prepared drywall may not grip a glossy cabinet or flexible plastic.

Before choosing paint, ask:

1. What is the surface actually made from?
2. Is it clean, dry, stable, and sound?
3. What exposure will it receive—water, sunlight, abrasion, heat, or handling?
4. Is there an existing finish, and will the new system adhere to it?

Our [surface-preparation guide](/surface-preparation-before-painting/) turns those questions into a practical inspection.

## What preparation does

Preparation is not one ritual for every job. It can include cleaning grease, removing loose coating, repairing damage, dulling gloss, smoothing defects, controlling dust, and protecting adjacent surfaces. The goal is a surface that is sound and appropriate for the next material.

Sanding is useful when a surface needs smoothing or mechanical tooth, but it is not automatically required everywhere. Cleaning usually comes first. Unknown older coatings can create lead or other hazards when disturbed, so identify risk before sanding.

## What primer does—and does not do

Primer is a preparatory coating used for a reason: it may seal porosity, improve bonding, isolate stains or tannins, protect certain substrates, or create a consistent base. It does not replace cleaning or repair, and “paint and primer in one” does not solve every adhesion or stain problem. Use our [primer decision guide](/do-you-need-primer/) for a project-specific answer.

## Basic ways to apply paint

- **Brushes** control edges, details, profiles, and expressive marks.
- **Rollers** cover broad architectural surfaces with a repeatable texture.
- **Sprayers** atomize a coating for fast, uniform coverage but require skill, masking, ventilation, and careful film control.
- **Palette knives, sponges, and other tools** create artistic marks or decorative effects.

The applicator affects texture, coverage, and speed. It cannot make an incompatible coating suitable for the surface.

## Coats, coverage, and film thickness

A coat is one complete layer. Multiple controlled coats often cover and cure more reliably than one heavy layer. Hiding the old color and building the coating's intended protective film are related but not identical: a surface may look uniformly colored before it has the specified film.

Follow the product's spread rate and recoat instructions. See [how many coats of paint you need](/how-many-coats-of-paint/) for the variables that change the answer.

## Drying is not the same as curing

Paint usually passes through touch-dry, recoat-ready, and cured stages. A wall that no longer feels wet may still mark under pressure, and a cabinet coating may need much longer before hard use. Temperature, humidity, airflow, thickness, and chemistry all affect timing. The label provides the relevant schedule; our [dry-time guide](/paint-dry-recoat-cure-time/) explains how to read it.

## Finish and sheen

Finish describes the completed surface and can also refer to a product's appearance. **Sheen** is how strongly it reflects light. Flatter wall finishes tend to hide surface variation; glossier finishes can be easier to wipe but highlight defects. Wear, moisture, appearance, and manufacturer recommendations should drive the choice—not a single room-by-room rule. Start with the [paint finish guide](/paint-finish-guide/).

## What is artistic painting?

Art painting organizes marks and color on a support such as canvas, paper, wood panel, or wall. Three common beginner media are:

- **Acrylic:** water-cleanup while wet, quick drying, and suitable for layered work on properly prepared surfaces.
- **Oil:** long working time and rich blending, with different solvent, ventilation, cleanup, and curing considerations.
- **Watercolor:** transparent color that uses the light of the paper and rewards control of water and timing.

None is the one correct starting medium. Acrylic is convenient for many beginners because a [small first-canvas setup](/acrylic-painting-for-beginners/) makes experimentation straightforward.

## Common painting terms

- **Coverage or hiding:** how well the coating obscures what is below.
- **Adhesion:** how firmly one layer attaches to another.
- **Wet edge:** the workable boundary where new paint can merge with the previous section.
- **Recoat time:** the minimum interval before another coat under stated conditions.
- **Cure:** development of the coating's intended hardness and performance.
- **Medium:** in art, both a type of material (such as acrylic) and, in another sense, a product mixed with paint to change its handling.
- **Ground:** a prepared layer on an art support; gesso is a common example.
