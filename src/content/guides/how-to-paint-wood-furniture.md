A lasting furniture finish begins with identifying what you actually have. Solid wood, wood veneer, and wood-look laminate can resemble one another but need different preparation. This guide covers sound solid wood or veneered wood with an existing finish.

## Check the piece before painting

Repair loose joints, water damage, and failing veneer first. Paint is not structural repair. Avoid casually sanding an old or unknown coating; vintage furniture may have hazardous paint or finishes. Test or seek qualified advice when its age or history raises concern.

Decide where the piece will live and how it will be used. A decorative shelf has different wear and moisture demands from a dining tabletop. Choose a coating approved for the material and use.

## 1. Remove hardware and label it

Take off pulls, knobs, doors, and removable shelves when doing so makes the work cleaner. Photograph complicated hardware before disassembly. Put screws in labeled bags so every part returns to the same location.

## 2. Clean thoroughly

Dust is only the first layer. Remove wax, polish, skin oils, cooking residue, and grime with a cleaner compatible with the existing finish. Residual furniture polish is a common source of adhesion trouble.

Rinse when directed and let the piece dry. Keep water away from vulnerable veneer edges and joints.

## 3. Repair and scuff-sand

Fill unwanted holes and dents with a compatible filler. Sand repairs level once dry. Then scuff-sand the sound finish evenly to remove gloss and give the next coating a consistent surface.

You usually do not need to strip a firmly attached finish to bare wood. Stop if the old coating gums up, flakes extensively, or reacts unexpectedly; that indicates a larger compatibility or condition problem.

Vacuum profiles, corners, and the floor around the work. Wipe away remaining dust using the coating manufacturer's recommended method.

## 4. Prime for a reason

Use a suitable primer when you expose bare wood, need to block tannins or stains, are making a dramatic color change, or need improved bonding to a difficult sound finish. Some woods bleed through water-based coatings, so a stain-blocking system may be necessary.

Apply primer thinly and evenly. After it dries, lightly smooth raised fibers or dust nibs if the instructions allow, then clean off dust.

## 5. Apply controlled finish coats

Support the piece so you can reach edges without resting a wet face on the floor. Start with profiles and inside corners, then coat broad surfaces. A small smooth-surface roller can make large panels more even; tip off or brush details before paint begins to set.

Two thin coats usually behave better than one heavy coat. Heavy paint collects along lower edges, fills crisp details, and stays soft longer. Follow recoat times and temperature requirements on the label.

> **Beginner tip:** Keep checking undersides and corners for runs during the first few minutes, then leave the coating alone once it begins to tack.

## 6. Let it cure before hard use

Reinstall hardware carefully after the coating is adequately dry. Avoid tightening hardware so aggressively that it twists soft paint. Leave doors and drawers open or use temporary spacers if painted edges might stick.

Wait for the stated cure period before placing heavy objects, wiping aggressively, or using the surface normally. Use pads beneath objects on frequently used tops.

## Do you need a clear topcoat?

Not automatically. A durable furniture or cabinet paint may be designed to stand alone. Adding a clear coating introduces another compatibility, color, and repair consideration. Use one only when the paint system recommends it or the project's wear demands justify it. Confirm compatibility and test first—some clear finishes can alter pale colors.

## Common mistakes

- Painting over polish or wax.
- Assuming veneer is thick enough for aggressive sanding.
- Choosing wall paint for a hard-wearing tabletop.
- Closing drawers before painted contact points have cured.
- Treating “dry to touch” as “ready for daily use.”
