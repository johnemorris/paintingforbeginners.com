The easiest way to make spray paint run is to try to make the first coat look finished.

Do not.

Spray paint usually looks better when you build the color with several light coats. Your first pass may look thin. Some of the old color may still show through. That is normal.

## Start by reading the can

Check the label for recommended spraying distance, temperature limits, shaking instructions, recoat time, surface preparation, ventilation, ignition hazards, and protective equipment.

The exact instructions vary by product, so the can takes priority over a universal number from an article. Work only where the label permits, protect the surrounding area, and test the spray on scrap before aiming at the project.

## Keep the can the right distance away

Many general-purpose spray paints are used roughly a foot from the surface, but follow the distance printed on your particular can.

Too close and you concentrate a lot of wet paint in one spot. Too far away and less paint reaches the surface properly, which can leave thin, rough, or uneven coverage.

## Start moving before you cover the surface

Do not point the can at one spot and hold the nozzle down while you decide where to move.

Use a smooth sweeping motion. Begin the spray just before the pass reaches the area being painted, move across it at a steady speed, and continue past the far edge before ending the pass.

## Overlap your passes

Let each pass overlap part of the previous spray pattern. You are trying to build one even coat, not a row of separate stripes.

Keep the can oriented as directed and maintain a consistent distance through the sweep. Swinging in an arc moves the can closer at the middle and farther away at the ends.

## Use light coats

After the first coat, you may think: **I can still see through this.**

That does not mean you should immediately pile more paint onto the same wet area.

A heavy coat can look covered for a moment and then begin to sag. Let the coat dry or recoat according to the product instructions, then build coverage with the next coat. [Learn how to judge whether a coat has enough paint](/how-do-i-know-if-i-put-on-enough-paint/) without forcing complete coverage too early.

## If the paint starts running

A run is a strong clue that too much wet paint has collected in that area.

Common reasons include holding the can too close, moving too slowly, pausing over one spot, repeatedly spraying an already-wet area, or trying to get full coverage in one coat.

Do not keep spraying the run in an attempt to make it disappear. Follow the product's drying instructions. Once appropriately dry, a run can often be leveled carefully and recoated rather than buried under more wet paint. The same workable-versus-setting decision applies when deciding [whether to go back over wet paint](/should-you-go-back-over-wet-paint/).

## The beginner rhythm

Think:

**shake → test → sweep → overlap → light coat → wait → repeat**

Not:

**point → soak it until covered**
