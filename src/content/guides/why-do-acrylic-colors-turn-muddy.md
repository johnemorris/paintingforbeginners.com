“Muddy” color usually means you mixed away the color contrast you wanted.

That can happen on the palette or directly on the canvas. The most common beginner causes are mixing too many colors together, using a dirty brush, repeatedly working wet layers, or blending complementary or opposing colors farther than intended.

## Start with fewer colors

If a mixture is not working, adding three more colors rarely makes it clearer.

Begin with two colors. Observe what they make. Adjust deliberately.

When you combine many pigments without a plan, the mixture often becomes less saturated and more neutral.

## Clean the brush when the mixture matters

A brush carrying leftover blue into a yellow mixture may create green. A brush carrying several previous colors into a light mixture may gray it immediately.

You do not need to sterilize the brush between every stroke, but when you need a clean color relationship, rinse and blot first.

## Let layers dry when you want separation

One advantage of acrylic is that a dry layer generally stays in place while you paint over it.

If you want a clean yellow detail over a blue area, allowing the blue to dry can be easier than trying to keep the colors separate while both are wet.

If you want a soft transition, work wet into wet intentionally.

## Mud is not always a mistake

Neutral browns, grays, and muted colors are useful. Landscapes and shadows often need them.

The problem is not that the color is neutral. The problem is losing control of the mixture.

## Make a small test mixture

Before changing a large area of the painting, mix a small amount on the palette. If it moves in the wrong direction, you have lost a small amount of paint instead of an entire sky.
