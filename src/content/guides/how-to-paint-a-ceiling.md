Ceilings are physically awkward, but the painting sequence is familiar: make the surface sound, protect the room, brush the edges, and roll before those edges dry. The biggest differences are reach, lighting, and the need to control drips above you.

## Decide whether the ceiling is ready

Fix active moisture before painting. A water mark with an unresolved leak will return, and a stain may bleed through ordinary finish paint even after the source is repaired. Once the ceiling is dry and sound, clean off dust and cobwebs, fill damage, sand repairs, and use an appropriate stain-blocking or spot primer when needed.

Texture changes the job. Do not aggressively scrub or sand an unfamiliar textured ceiling. Older ceiling materials and coatings can present hazards; when age or condition raises doubt, have the material evaluated before disturbing it.

## 1. Clear and cover the whole work zone

Remove as much furniture as possible. Cover everything that remains and protect the full floor, not only the area beneath the roller. Fine droplets travel farther than expected.

Remove light shades only when you can do so safely and the power is appropriately controlled. Otherwise, protect fixtures without creating heat or electrical hazards. Never paint a warm fixture.

## 2. Arrange safe reach

Use a roller extension pole long enough to reach while standing upright with relaxed shoulders. More length is not always better; a pole that flexes or feels unwieldy is hard to control.

Use only a stable ladder rated and positioned for the task. Keep your belt buckle between the rails and climb down to reposition it instead of leaning. Do not stand on furniture, boxes, or improvised platforms. High, vaulted, stairwell, damaged, or difficult-access ceilings are reasonable places to hire qualified help.

## 3. Control the paint

Stir the paint thoroughly. Load the roller until the cover is evenly filled but not dripping. Roll it across the tray ramp or grid rather than pressing out most of the paint.

Wear eye protection. Move slowly when lifting the loaded roller overhead, and do not start directly above your face.

## 4. Cut in, then roll a section

Brush a band along one manageable stretch of wall, around fixtures, and into corners. Roll that area while the brushed paint remains wet enough to blend. Cutting in the entire ceiling first can leave a visible dry border.

Start near the room's main natural-light source and roll away from it in overlapping strips. This makes minor roller lines less obvious in grazing daylight. Work in sections roughly 3–4 feet wide and keep a wet edge.

Finish each section with light passes in one direction. Heavy pressure squeezes paint from the roller ends and creates ridges. Reload instead of forcing a dry cover to stretch farther.

## 5. Inspect from the floor

Look from several angles with daylight and the room lights on. Do not chase every dull-looking wet patch; wet ceiling paint reflects unevenly. Let the coat dry for the full label time before deciding whether coverage is patchy.

A second coat is often worthwhile, especially after repairs, color change, or uneven porosity. Apply it in a direction perpendicular to the first when practical so minor misses are less likely to repeat.

## Common ceiling mistakes

- Using a dry roller and pressing hard.
- Choosing a cover too short for the ceiling texture.
- Rolling back into paint that has started to set.
- Treating an active moisture stain as only a color problem.
- Overreaching rather than moving the ladder.
- Removing floor protection before the brush and tray leave the room.

## Cleanup

Bring tools down carefully and wipe drips while they are still compatible with the cleanup method on the label. Clean or dispose of tools according to the coating directions and local rules. Let the ceiling cure before exposing it to heavy bathroom humidity or other demanding conditions when the product instructions call for that delay.
