For most beginner wall painting, the useful answer is:

**Cut in a manageable section, then roll that section before the brushed edge dries.**

That is more important than arguing about whether every brush stroke must technically happen before every roller stroke.

## Why not cut in the entire room first?

Brushed paint can dry before you return with the roller.

Because brushing and rolling leave different textures and can apply different film thicknesses, a completely dry cut-in band may remain visible around the edges of the wall.

Working in smaller sections helps the two application methods blend.

## A simple one-person workflow

For each wall:

1. brush the ceiling line, trim edges, and corners you need for a manageable section
2. load the roller
3. roll close to the brushed area while it is still workable
4. finish the wall section consistently
5. continue to the next section

For a small wall, that may mean cutting the whole wall before rolling. For a large wall, break it into smaller working areas.

## Should the roller touch the cut-in?

Usually, yes—slightly.

Roll as close to the boundary as you safely can and overlap some of the brushed paint. You are trying to avoid leaving a wide band of brush-only texture.

Do not roll into the ceiling, trim, or adjacent color just to eliminate every trace of brushing.

## What if you are painting alone?

That is exactly why section size matters.

You do not need professional speed. Make the section small enough that **you** can cut and roll it comfortably.

## What about the second coat?

Use the same disciplined sequence.

A second coat does not excuse a first coat full of ridges, drips, or heavy dried edges. Let the first coat dry for the required recoat interval, correct defects if needed, then apply the next coat uniformly.

## The rule worth remembering

Do not organize the job around finishing all the brush work first.

Organize it around **keeping adjacent paint workable long enough to blend**.
