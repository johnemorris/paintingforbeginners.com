Framing begins with the material beneath the paint. A stretched canvas, thin canvas panel, work on paper, and rigid wood panel should not be treated as interchangeable. Choose the frame and mounting method only after identifying the support and measuring it accurately.

## Know what you are framing

- **Stretched canvas** is fabric wrapped around wooden stretcher bars. It has depth and may be gallery-wrapped, with finished painted edges.
- **Canvas panel or board** is canvas adhered to a thin rigid board. It needs support within a frame and does not hang like a stretched canvas.
- **Painting on paper** normally benefits from a mat or spacers, backing, and glazing that keeps the painted surface from touching the glass or acrylic.
- **Rigid panel** may be wood, hardboard, or another prepared board. Its weight, thickness, and response to humidity affect mounting.

## Does a stretched canvas need a frame?

No. A neatly wrapped canvas with finished edges can be displayed unframed. A frame can add visual separation, protect vulnerable edges, or coordinate with a room, but it is not required simply because the work is on canvas.

Do not place ordinary glazing directly over an acrylic or oil painting on stretched canvas. Paintings generally need air space and a mounting system designed for the medium. Conservation needs vary, so valuable or fragile work belongs with a professional framer.

## Standard frame or floating frame?

A **standard frame** overlaps the front edge of the artwork with a lip called a rabbet. It can conceal staples and imperfect canvas edges but must be deep enough for the stretcher.

A **floating frame** holds a stretched canvas with a narrow visible gap around it, making the artwork appear to float. It leaves the full face visible and accommodates depth, but the spacing must be even.

Canvas panels often fit a standard frame intended for panels or may be secured within a floater using an appropriate mounting board. Do not improvise with adhesive directly on artwork you may want to preserve.

## Measure before buying

Measure width and height at several points; handmade canvases may not be perfectly square. Record the maximum dimensions and the depth. Frame sizes usually refer to the opening or artwork size, not the outside dimensions.

Also check:

- how much of the face the frame lip will cover;
- whether the rabbet is deep enough;
- clearance needed for a floating gap;
- location of stretcher bars and existing hardware;
- final frame weight and hanging method.

When ordering a custom frame, follow the seller's measurement convention exactly. “About 16 by 20” is not precise enough.

## Frame a stretched canvas

1. Place the clean frame face-down on a padded surface.
2. Lower the dry, cured canvas into it without dragging the painted face.
3. Center it and check the reveal from the front.
4. Attach it with canvas offset clips, screws through approved frame brackets, or the frame maker's hardware. Never drive a screw through the canvas fabric.
5. Add hanging hardware sized for the completed weight.

Avoid permanently gluing a stretched canvas into a frame. Removable mechanical attachment makes adjustment and future reframing possible.

## Frame a canvas panel, paper painting, or board

A thin panel may sit behind the lip of a standard frame with a rigid backing and suitable retaining points. Do not crush or flex it. Works on paper commonly use an archival-quality mat or spacers to prevent direct contact with glazing; hinges should be conservation-appropriate and reversible.

Glazing can protect paper from dust and handling. Acrylic glazing is lighter and more impact-resistant than glass, while glass can resist scratching better. UV-filtering products reduce some light exposure but do not make direct sun safe.

## Choose a frame visually

Let the painting lead. A quiet frame can separate a busy picture from the wall; a wider frame can give a small work more presence. Compare samples beside the actual art under its display lighting. Color need not match exactly—a related neutral or restrained contrast often works better.

## When professional framing makes sense

Use a professional framer for valuable, sentimental, unusually large, heavy, warped, fragile, or historically important work; for unfamiliar media; or when conservation mounting and glazing matter. Ask for reversible methods and materials appropriate to the medium.
