Trim is close to floors, corners, hardware, and other finished surfaces, so flaws show differently than they do on a broad wall. The key is not an impossibly steady hand; it is a clean, smooth surface and a sequence that prevents you from repeatedly brushing half-dry paint.

## Check the existing condition

Push gently on loose trim and repair attachment problems before finishing. Look for peeling paint, separated joints, dents, and gaps. Determine whether a gap is meant to move: some seams can be caulked, while door clearances and intentional joints must remain free.

Older painted trim can contain lead. Do not casually dry-sand or scrape an unknown coating in an older home. Use current lead-safe guidance or qualified testing when appropriate.

## 1. Clean where hands and vacuums leave residue

Baseboards collect dust, floor-cleaner residue, pet hair, and grime. Door and window trim collects skin oils. Vacuum first, then clean with a product compatible with the old finish. Rinse if directed and let the trim dry.

Painting over dust produces roughness. Painting over polish or grease risks poor adhesion.

## 2. Fill, caulk, and sand selectively

Set protruding nail heads. Fill unwanted nail holes and dents with material intended for painted trim. Use paintable caulk only at appropriate stationary gaps; smooth it without spreading a thin film far across the face.

When repairs are dry, sand them flush and scuff glossy sound paint to a consistently dull finish. Use light pressure on detailed profiles and edges. Vacuum and wipe away the residue.

## 3. Prime problem areas

Spot-prime bare wood, filler, and exposed patches. Use a suitable stain blocker where knots or tannins are likely to bleed. If the old coating is very glossy or compatibility is uncertain, the prepared surface may need a bonding primer approved for it.

Primer does not replace cleaning or removal of loose paint.

## 4. Protect adjacent surfaces

Slide a clean trim guard or firm piece of thin cardboard beneath a removable shoe molding only if it can be moved without damaging the floor. Otherwise use floor protection and carefully applied painter's tape where helpful.

Tape bonds best to clean, dry surfaces. Press down the working edge. Do not assume tape can stay indefinitely; follow its removal window.

## 5. Brush in short sections

Load only the lower portion of the bristles and tap off excess without wiping the brush nearly dry. Start slightly away from the edge, spread the paint, then guide it toward the line.

Complete a section a few feet long. Finish with light strokes along the length of the trim, then move forward while the edge remains wet. Avoid returning to a section that has begun to tack.

On baseboards, paint the top edge, face, and lower edge as a coordinated section. Check corners and the bottom lip for runs.

## 6. Recoat and remove protection carefully

Respect the label's recoat time. Lightly smooth dust nibs between coats only if the system permits, then remove all dust. A second thin coat is generally more even and durable than one heavy coat.

Remove tape according to its instructions. If paint bridges across the tape edge, score the film carefully so removal does not lift the new finish.

## Protect the cure

Dry trim can still be soft. Avoid washing, bumping with a vacuum, closing painted contact edges, or replacing furniture tightly against it until the coating has had suitable cure time.

Save the product name, color, sheen, and date. Trim receives frequent impacts, so accurate touch-up information is unusually useful.
